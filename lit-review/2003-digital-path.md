# The Digital Path: Smart Contracts and the Third World

- 2003 — Miller & Stiegler. ["The Digital Path: Smart Contracts and the Third World"](http://papers.agoric.com/papers/the-digital-path-smart-contracts-and-the-third-world/abstract/). _Markets, Information and Communication: Austrian Perspectives on the Internet Economy, Routledge._

## Abstract

Inadequate and ill-adapted property institutions in the third world prevent the extralegal assets of the poor from serving as capital. In particular, the absence of credible systems of title transfer makes real estate holdings ineffective as collateral for loans. How can this barrier to wealth creation be surmounted? Country-by-country institutional reform is possible, but inevitably slow. New options based on computer networks and trusted computational agents may provide a shorter path. By leveraging trust in first-world institutions while enabling the evolution of contractual arrangements that fit local needs and traditions, this approach could bring advanced property systems to regions now paralyzed by their absence.

## Summary

### Motivation: de Soto's mystery of capital

Drawing on Hernando de Soto's *The Mystery of Capital*, the paper observes that the world's poor hold vast informal assets (informally owned buildings alone were valued at $9.3 trillion) that fail to function as *capital* — collateral for loans, investment vehicles — because there is no credible system of **title transfer at a distance**. A house you cannot be evicted from is an asset; a mortgage on it would be capital; but a bank dares not accept it as collateral without credible, transferable property rights.

De Soto's proposed *governmental path* — reforming national legal systems and bureaucracies — is slow and politically opposed. This paper proposes an alternate **digital path** made possible by the falling cost of electronics and networks.

### The digital path: smart contracts

If binding contracts for ownership transfer lie at the heart of capital formation, what if traditional contracts were supplemented with **smart contracts** — contract-like arrangements expressed in program code, where the program's execution enforces the terms? Smart contracts enable cooperation among mutually suspicious parties, often without legal recourse, providing fine-grained, jurisdiction-free, automated arrangements for which legal contracts are impractical.

The paper synthesises de Soto with Francis Fukuyama's analysis of **trust networks**: large-scale trust depends on widely-trusted intermediary institutions ("trust hubs") — title registries, legal systems. The digital path proposes to *leverage existing first-world trust hubs* (whose credibility is already widely recognised) to short-circuit the slow process of building such institutions from scratch in the third world, while enabling contractual arrangements that fit local needs and traditions.

### Backing, legitimacy, and limitations

- **Backing:** The governmental path provides backing by coercive enforcement. The non-coercive digital path instead relies on **ratings** — independent estimates of the likelihood a title listing would be honoured, like credit reports for systems of local law — and **video contracts** to bridge the abstract digital world and local systems of largely unwritten arrangements.
- **Split contracts:** Smart contracts cannot express the subtle richness of natural-language contracts, so the paper proposes combining the two into *split contracts* with both program and natural-language elements.
- **Regulatory capture:** A naively deployed smart-contract system could replace vulnerability to local governments with vulnerability to distant governments; the paper discusses mitigations.

### Why the Third World first?

The Third World may lead the transition because: (1) its legitimacy models differ; (2) the first world has already paid the homogenisation costs the digital path could have preserved; (3) the Third World lacks a working installed base of law and property, enabling leapfrogging (cf. cell phones in Eastern Europe). Success there may then cause the first world to follow.
